## The Endeavor

My proposed endeavor is to build and scale open, field-tested curricula and applied AI/cybersecurity teaching tools that prepare community college and university students — including underrepresented and non-traditional learners such as military veterans and their spouses — for careers in artificial intelligence and operational technology (OT) security, and to do so through direct partnership with the national institutions responsible for setting that curriculum agenda across the United States.

Concretely, this means three things I intend to keep doing, not just things I have already done:

1. **Completing and launching a new undergraduate AI degree program** at City University of Seattle — the BS in Artificial Intelligence, spanning human-AI interaction, agentic systems, and MLOps — and publishing the curriculum design as a reusable model other institutions can adopt.
2. **Extending my open-access teaching tools beyond a single university.** My AI Hub platform (a production RAG and multi-agent system built on Google's Agent Developer Kit) and ScriptedOT (an OT/ICS security curriculum tool mapping real critical-infrastructure incidents to the Purdue Model and IEC 62443) are already built; the endeavor is to keep developing and distributing them through partnerships with national cybersecurity-education infrastructure, not to keep them CityU-internal.
3. **Deepening my work with NCyTE Center** — the NSF-funded National Cybersecurity Training & Education Center — where I have contributed to faculty-development workshops in 2025 and 2026 and am in active discussion about a formal collaboration on OT-security curriculum.

## Why This Matters Nationally

This endeavor addresses two documented, currently active national priorities — not a general sense that "AI is important."

**The cybersecurity workforce gap is specific and measured.** ISC2's 2024 Cybersecurity Workforce Study put the global gap at 4.8 million unfilled positions, with more than 500,000 of those in the United States alone. The 2025 follow-up study found the binding constraint has shifted from headcount to skills: 95% of surveyed organizations report at least one unmet skill need, and 59% call it a critical or significant gap. That is a training and curriculum problem before it is a hiring problem — exactly the level at which my work operates.

**The operational-technology security gap inside that shortage is narrower and more acute.** A March 2024 GAO report (GAO-24-106576) found that CISA — the federal agency responsible for defending the country's power grid, water systems, and manufacturing infrastructure — has only a handful of federal staff (reported as two to four across coverage of the same report, exact figure to confirm against the primary text) plus five contractors doing OT-specific threat hunting and incident response nationwide. CISA's own Executive Assistant Director for Cybersecurity has called hiring qualified ICS/OT security staff "a universally acknowledged challenge." Separately, the SANS 2024 State of ICS/OT Cybersecurity Report found more than half of the existing ICS workforce has under five years of experience, and 51% hold no formal cybersecurity credential at all. This is the gap ScriptedOT and my work with NCyTE Center directly target: training faculty and students in OT/ICS security before they enter the workforce, not after.

**Current federal policy treats both as active priorities, not background concerns.** The April 2025 executive order *Advancing Artificial Intelligence Education for American Youth* and the June 2025 order *Sustaining Select Efforts to Strengthen the Nation's Cybersecurity* both direct federal resources toward exactly this kind of early-pipeline technical education. The June 2026 executive order *Promoting Advanced Artificial Intelligence Innovation and Security* explicitly pairs AI innovation with securing the systems AI increasingly touches — the same intersection my OWASP-aligned agentic-AI threat-modeling work sits in. And the U.S. Tech Force initiative, launched December 2025 with an Information Cybersecurity Specialist track added in April 2026, is direct evidence the federal government is actively building hiring pipelines for the exact skill set this endeavor trains people toward.

NCyTE Center — the NSF-funded national center I already partner with — exists inside this same federal architecture: an NSF Advanced Technological Education National Center whose explicit mission is expanding cybersecurity educational pathways at the community-college level nationally. My work is not adjacent to national policy in this area; it operates inside the specific institutional structure the federal government has built to carry that policy out.

## Why a Waiver Serves the National Interest

*[Prong III. Dhanasar asks whether, on balance, the US benefits from waiving the job-offer and labor-certification requirements. The strongest version of this argument for someone in my position isn't "I'm irreplaceable" — it's structural: PERM tests the labor market for one job at one employer, and this endeavor was never shaped like that.]*

**Labor certification is built to test a single job at a single employer — this endeavor isn't that.** PERM requires an employer to prove no qualified US worker is available for one specific position. My endeavor doesn't reduce to one position: it already spans City University of Seattle (where I'm employed) and NCyTE Center (an independent NSF-funded national center whose workshops I've contributed to, not an employer), and the forward-looking plan explicitly extends it to the community colleges NCyTE serves nationally. There is no single employer whose job posting could capture what this work actually is, and forcing it through a PERM filing tied to one institution would artificially shrink the endeavor to fit the process, rather than the process serving the endeavor.

**The field-level data explains why flexibility matters more than a single hire would.** This isn't a claim that I'm impossible to replace — it's that the shortage this work addresses is a pipeline problem, not a staffing problem a single PERM filing could solve even in principle:
- The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects **29% employment growth for Information Security Analysts from 2024–2034** — more than seven times the average growth rate for all occupations — with about **16,000 openings projected annually** and a median wage of $124,910 (May 2024). *(U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Outlook Handbook)*
- The Department of Labor's O\*NET database — the federal government's own occupational classification system — designates Information Security Analysts (SOC 15-1212.00) a **"Bright Outlook: Rapid Growth"** occupation, its official designation for fields expected to add substantial numbers of jobs. *(O\*NET OnLine, U.S. Department of Labor/Employment and Training Administration)*
- This sits inside the broader shortage already documented in this statement's national-importance section — ISC2's measured 4.8 million global / 500,000+ US unfilled cybersecurity positions, and CISA's own acknowledgment that it has only two federal staff and five contractors handling OT-specific threat hunting nationwide.

Read together, these aren't evidence that one hire (me) is scarce — they're evidence that the *pipeline itself* is the bottleneck, which is exactly the level my endeavor operates at (training the next cohort of practitioners, not filling one role). A labor certification process built to test whether *a* qualified worker exists for *a* job doesn't map onto a shortage this structural.

**Even a successful PERM filing would work against the endeavor's actual goal.** If a labor certification process forced this work to be filed as one job at one employer, and by some measure a "qualified" US candidate were found for that single posting, the underlying pipeline problem — the 29% projected growth, the 16,000 annual unfilled openings — would be entirely unaffected. The national interest here isn't served by filling one position; it's served by the work continuing across institutions, unconstrained by a single employer's hiring cycle.
