NIW Talking Points

What to say when friends ask. Honest, easy to repeat, and light on the sensitive details.

The 15-second version
“It's a green card category where you make the case that your work benefits the US, so you don't need an employer to sponsor you. Mine's about AI and cybersecurity education: training students, community college faculty, and veterans for those careers.”
What's your case actually about?
“I've been building AI and security teaching tools and a new AI degree program, and working with a national cybersecurity education center. The pitch is that there's a real shortage of people trained in this, and what I'm building helps close it.”
Where are you in the process?
“Still in the build phase: gathering evidence and letters, getting the legal questions answered before I file anything. I'd rather file once and file well than rush it.”
True, and it doesn't commit you to a timeline.
How long does it take?
“Depends on the route. Anywhere from a few months to a couple of years once it's filed, and I'm still building the case first.”
Do you have a lawyer?
“Still weighing my options. I'm comparing a few before I commit.”
Don't name a firm or a price, even with close friends — it invites opinions and gets repeated.
Do you think you'll get it?
“I think the case is solid and getting stronger, but there are no guarantees, so I'm being careful about how I build it.”
Skip “definitely.” Confidence sounds fine now and awkward later.
They ask about your status or history
“Long story on the status side. I'll tell you over coffee sometime, but the short version is I'm working on a plan.”
Keep the details for people you'd trust with them.
They offer to help
“Honestly, the most useful thing is if you know a good immigration attorney. Happy to take a referral, even just for a one-time consult.”
The best standing answer — it turns goodwill into something useful.
Horror stories or unsolicited advice
“Thanks, that's good to know. Every case is different, so I'm getting it looked at by someone who can see my specifics.”

What to avoid

  • Who you're asking for letters. Friends may know the same people, and you don't want a reference hearing about the ask secondhand.
  • Any work under double-blind review. Publicizing it during review can reveal your identity to reviewers. Keep it out of casual talk and social media until decisions are in.
  • Money and fees.
  • Odds, or what “they” told you. Anything a liaison or friend said about your chances isn't something to repeat as fact.
Personal reference. Say it in your own words — these are starting points, not a script.
