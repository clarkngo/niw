The endeavor

What this statement is about

My proposed endeavor is to build and scale open, field-tested curricula and applied AI/cybersecurity teaching tools that prepare community college and university students — including underrepresented and non-traditional learners such as military veterans and their spouses — for careers in artificial intelligence and operational technology (OT) security, and to do so through direct partnership with the national institutions responsible for setting that curriculum agenda across the United States.

Concretely, this means three things I intend to keep doing, not just things I have already done:

  1. Completing and launching a new undergraduate AI degree program at City University of Seattle — the BS in Artificial Intelligence, spanning human-AI interaction, agentic systems, and MLOps — and publishing the curriculum design as a reusable model other institutions can adopt.
  2. Extending my open-access teaching tools beyond a single university. AI Hub (a production RAG and multi-agent system on Google's Agent Developer Kit) and ScriptedOT (an OT/ICS security curriculum tool mapping real critical-infrastructure incidents to the Purdue Model and IEC 62443) are already built; the endeavor is to keep distributing them through national cybersecurity-education infrastructure, not to keep them CityU-internal.
  3. Deepening my work with NCyTE Center — the NSF-funded National Cybersecurity Training & Education Center — where I have contributed to faculty-development workshops in 2025 and 2026 and am in active discussion about a formal collaboration on OT-security curriculum.
Prong I Substantial merit & national importance

Why this matters nationally

This endeavor addresses two documented, currently active national priorities — not a general sense that "AI is important."

The cybersecurity workforce gap is specific and measured. ISC2's 2024 study put the global gap at 4.8 million unfilled positions, with more than 500,000 in the US alone. The 2025 follow-up found the binding constraint has shifted from headcount to skills: 95% of organizations report at least one unmet skill need. That's a training and curriculum problem before it's a hiring problem — exactly the level this work operates at.

The operational-technology security gap inside that shortage is narrower and more acute. A March 2024 GAO report (GAO-24-106576) found that CISA — the federal agency defending the country's power grid, water systems, and manufacturing infrastructure — has only a handful of federal staff doing OT-specific threat hunting and incident response nationwide, plus five contractors. CISA's own Executive Assistant Director for Cybersecurity has called hiring qualified ICS/OT staff "a universally acknowledged challenge." This is the gap ScriptedOT and my work with NCyTE Center directly target.

Current federal policy treats both as active priorities. EO 14277, Advancing AI Education for American Youth (Apr. 2025) and EO 14306, Sustaining Select Efforts to Strengthen the Nation's Cybersecurity (Jun. 2025) direct federal resources toward exactly this kind of early-pipeline technical education. EO 14409, Promoting Advanced AI Innovation and Security (Jun. 2026) pairs AI innovation with securing the systems AI touches — the same intersection my OWASP-aligned threat-modeling work sits in. And the U.S. Tech Force initiative is direct evidence the federal government is building hiring pipelines for this exact skill set.

NCyTE Center — the NSF-funded national center I already partner with — exists inside this same federal architecture. My work is not adjacent to national policy here; it operates inside the specific institutional structure the federal government has built to carry that policy out.

Prong III Beneficial to waive the job offer

Why a waiver serves the national interest

The strongest version of this argument for someone in my position isn't "I'm irreplaceable" — it's structural: PERM tests the labor market for one job at one employer, and this endeavor was never shaped like that.

Labor certification is built to test a single job at a single employer — this endeavor isn't that. My endeavor doesn't reduce to one position: it already spans City University of Seattle (where I'm employed) and NCyTE Center (an independent NSF-funded national center whose workshops I've contributed to, not an employer), and the forward-looking plan explicitly extends it to the community colleges NCyTE serves nationally. Forcing this through a PERM filing tied to one institution would artificially shrink the endeavor to fit the process, rather than the process serving the endeavor.

The field-level data explains why flexibility matters more than a single hire would — this isn't a claim that I'm impossible to replace, it's that the shortage is a pipeline problem, not a staffing problem a single PERM filing could solve even in principle:
